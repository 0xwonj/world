# Caves of Qud Reference Notes

Caves of Qud is a useful reference because it combines traditional roguelike
mechanics with a dense systemic world model.

## Important Lessons

### Hand-authored world plus procedural history

The game does not feel like a purely random map generator. It combines authored
lore, locations, quests, factions, and tone with procedural histories, ruins,
items, secrets, and encounters.

Useful lesson: procedural generation should create context, not just layout.

### Character build as action-space design

Mutations, cybernetics, skills, castes, equipment, and body changes are not just
stat modifiers. They change what the character can perceive, survive, equip,
traverse, attack, escape, or negotiate with.

Useful lesson: abilities should expand or reshape available actions.

### Body and entity symmetry

Creatures are not merely combat stat blocks. Body parts, equipment slots,
conditions, faction identity, and abilities make entities part of the same
simulation grammar as the player.

Useful lesson: NPCs and players should share as much simulation machinery as
possible.

### Materials and physical affordances

Walls, liquids, gases, temperature, acid, fire, electricity, phase, and
durability are part of the tactical language.

Useful lesson: terrain and objects should be interactive matter, not just map
decoration.

### Factions and reputation

Social relationships create non-combat solutions and surprising consequences.
Hostility is not just a property of a monster type; it can depend on reputation,
group identity, local context, past actions, and knowledge.

Useful lesson: faction relations should feed into perception, dialogue,
combat, trade, and alliance behavior.

### Information as gameplay currency

Secrets, rumors, locations, histories, and identities can matter as much as
items. The player can learn, trade, or exploit information.

Useful lesson: knowledge should be modeled as gameplay state, not only as
flavor text.
